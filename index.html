Maren Hoff
Harvard Business School
Golden Years or Gray Fears? Consumer Responses to Aging Portrayals
Abstract

This research examines how aging portrayals in the marketplace influence consumer responses across industries. Through a multi-method approach combining longitudinal analyses of implicit associations with older adults, text analyses of reviews for reality TV shows, and nine experiments, we demonstrate that portrayals of older individuals in marketing communications and media positively influence consumers’ reactions toward product offerings. These effects are driven by peace of mind about aging: a sense of internal peace and harmony regarding one’s own aging process. The impact of aging portrayals varies by product domain and consumer characteristics. In cognitive and romantic domains (e.g., education, entertainment, dating), both men and women respond favorably. In contrast, in beauty domains, where appearance-related aging anxieties are salient, aging portrayals increase peace of mind about aging more among women than men, reflecting gendered ageism. We further identify a boundary condition: portrayals of ungraceful aging that appear unnatural and/or unattractive elicit lower peace of mind about aging and more negative reactions than portrayals of graceful aging. These findings offer a novel framework for positioning aging not as a marker of decline but as a source of peace of mind, challenging internalized ageism and expanding identity-relevant marketing strategies.

Tong Wang
Yale School of Management
From What Works to Why It Works: Knowledge-Guided Fine-Tuning for Content Generation
Abstract

Firms increasingly fine-tune generative AI on A/B test data to produce marketing content (headlines, subject lines, advertisements) that resonates with audiences. But fine-tuning on outcomes teaches models what content performs well without teaching them why, producing reward hacking: in our setting, standard fine-tuning inflates “shocking” from 0.7% to 43.7% of generated headlines and independently amplifies hyperbolic language and narrows vocabulary. We propose knowledge-guided alignment, which conditions fine-tuning on a structured knowledge block K encoding validated behavioral hypotheses about why content engages (such as curiosity gaps, protagonist framing, or audience-relevant specificity). While the DPO temperature β constrains how far the model moves from its reference policy, K constrains where it moves, toward hypothesis-consistent generation. K is discovered endogenously from the A/B data: a reasoning LLM proposes candidate hypotheses from small mini-batches of preference pairs, each candidate is validated by its effect on generation quality across the full training distribution, and a combinatorial search selects the optimal set. Across 23,437 A/B-tested headlines, knowledge-guided alignment is preferred by human evaluators 38% vs. 31.5% for standard fine-tuning (p < 0.001) and reduces clickbait, hyperbole, and vocabulary collapse without targeting any explicitly. The gains are largest when training data is scarce. No alternative (human expertise, zero-shot LLM knowledge, four state-of-the-art hypothesis generators, or direct clickbait penalization) achieves simultaneous improvement across all evaluation dimensions.

Jacqueline R. Rifkin
Cornell University
Best Laid Plans: The Hidden Cost of a “Planned” Apology
Abstract

In this “age of apology,” public figures are increasingly held to account and, in turn, offer apologies. These figures are routinely advised to carefully plan and prepare their apologies, yet many of these apologies fail. Across several studies, including tightly controlled experiments and an analysis of real YouTube apologies and their comments, we identify one reason: the perception that an apology was planned. We find that apologies that are perceived as involving more (vs. less) intentional deliberation prior to delivery are less forgiven because they seem less sincere. This appears to stem from a lay belief that, despite being viewed positively in many life domains, planning in this context reflects impression management motives. We further identify contextual and nonverbal cues that observers use to infer planning, as well as identify an intervention that curtails these negative effects: signaling that pre-apology planning was oriented toward repair, rather than impression management. These findings highlight the importance of process cues—i.e., inferences of how actions come to be—in consumer evaluations, in addition to previously studied cues such as apology content or timing.

Ruru Hoong
MIT Sloan School of Management
Calibrated Coarsening: Designing Information for AI-Assisted Decisions
Abstract

Artificial intelligence (AI) is increasingly used to aid human decision-making across critical applications, but human cognitive biases (e.g., errors in probabilistic reasoning) often undermine its effectiveness. This raises the question of how to provide AI input to humans in a way that improves decision-making outcomes. We propose calibrated coarsening—partitioning the signal space into fewer cells in a manner calibrated to the decision environment rather than chosen arbitrarily—as a way to do so that (i) retains humans’ final decision rights, (ii) modifies signals without deception, and (iii) improves outcomes under a range of empirically common biases. Within an information disclosure framework, we derive an approximately optimal binary coarsened policy when the designer does not observe the decision-maker’s information. In an experiment with professional loan specialists (N = 150), we empirically demonstrate that coarsening AI signals at the theory-motivated threshold improves decision accuracy, outperforming both the human-only (based solely on the loan application) and uncoarsened AI assistance (continuous AI risk-score, in addition to loan application) benchmarks. We uncover substantial heterogeneity among specialists and estimate a Bayesian hierarchical latent-variable model (HLM), which we use to simulate and test the benefits of personalized coarsening.

Lauren Grewal
Dartmouth College
Normative Effects in Group Decisions: The Role of the First Follower
Abstract

Individuals frequently make individual choices in social or public settings. This research identifies a novel key decision-maker in setting group norms where people make individual consumption decisions: the first follower (second person) relative to the leader (first person). We argue that when the first follower makes a decision similar to the leader, the group norm will be to choose similarly, and when the first follower makes a decision different from the leader, group members will follow a norm of choosing differently. This has implications for variety-seeking behavior (e.g., food choices) and consumer wellbeing decisions (e.g., caloric intake and spending). Using a multimethod approach, we demonstrate our effect across two field studies at restaurants and five controlled experiments. We additionally show the process by which our effect occurs (group identification), highlighting that our effect is a type of value-expressive social influence (vs. other possible influences that have been shown to influence decision making), and use this to derive relevant boundary conditions for our effect and differentiate our effect from previously studied research on norms and decision-making in groups.
